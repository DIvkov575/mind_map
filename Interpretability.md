Understanding *why* a model produced a given output — splits into two genuinely different methodologies, easy to conflate but answering different questions:

**Behavioral / black-box**: treat the model as a system you can only probe from the outside — read its chain-of-thought, causally intervene on the *environment* or *prompt* (not the weights) and see what changes, sample alternative continuations. [[Model Forensics]] is this approach: hypothesis generation from CoT, sentence-resampling to measure a sentence's causal effect on downstream behavior, user-turn sampling — all done without ever opening up the model's internals.

**Mechanistic**: open up the model's internals directly — activations, weights, individual features. [[Sparse Autoencoders (SAE)|Sparse Autoencoders]] decompose a layer's activations into a larger set of sparse, more monosemantic features than the raw neurons offer, under the [[Linear Representation Hypothesis]] that concepts are linear directions in activation space. [[J-Space]]'s "Jlense" technique is a concrete mechanistic-interpretability method in this vein — linearize each layer's effect on the final logits (averaged across many prompts to strip out context-specific behavior) and read off what a given activation (a residual-stream vector, an SAE feature) is "poised to make the model say."

Behavioral methods can generate hypotheses cheaply at the level of "what circumstances trigger this behavior"; mechanistic methods can (in principle) verify *how* the behavior is actually computed inside the network — but require far more machinery to even get started.
