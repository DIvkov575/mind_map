The $d$-dimensional volume of a set — the specific instance of [[Lebesgue Measure]] $\mathcal{L}_d$ for a given $d$ (length at $d{=}1$, area at $d{=}2$, ordinary volume at $d{=}3$, and so on for higher $d$), regardless of what ambient dimension $D \geq d$ the set actually sits in.

- a $d$-dimensional submanifold embedded in $\mathbb{R}^D$ (e.g. a curve or surface in 3-space) has a well-defined $d$-volume even though it has zero $D$-volume (a curve has zero area, a surface has zero ordinary volume)
- this is the notion of "size" that matters for the [[Curse of Dimensionality]]: covering a $d$-dimensional manifold with balls of radius $\epsilon$ takes $\Theta(\epsilon^{-d})$ of them — governed by the manifold's own $d$-volume/dimension, not the ambient $D$
