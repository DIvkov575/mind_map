An adhesive is a material applied between two substrates to bind them and resist separation.

A useful joint model has four stages:

1. **Wetting:** liquid adhesive spreads across the substrate and enters surface irregularities.
2. **Adhesion:** mechanical interlocking and molecular interactions hold adhesive to each substrate.
3. **Cohesion:** the adhesive's own polymer network carries load across the joint.
4. **Hardening:** cooling, solvent/water loss, or chemical curing raises the adhesive's strength.

Failure location distinguishes two common modes:

- **Adhesive failure:** separation occurs at the adhesive–substrate interface.
- **Cohesive failure:** fracture propagates through the adhesive itself.

Good joint design requires compatible surface energies, clean substrates, adequate contact pressure, and enough bonded area. Smooth polymers may need roughening, plasma/corona treatment, flame treatment, or a primer to improve wetting.

**EVA on polyester film**

[[Ethylene-Vinyl Acetate (EVA)]] hot melts are thermoplastics: heating lowers viscosity so the adhesive wets the surface, then cooling solidifies the joint. Vinyl-acetate content improves polarity relative to polyethylene, but bonding to [[PET]] or [[Mylar]] still depends on the specific formulation and surface preparation. A thin heat-seal coating is usually more controllable than a thick glue-gun bead for film seams.
