The Lie bracket records the leading-order failure of two infinitesimal transformations to commute. It returns a direction, not a scalar amount of noncommutativity. Assigning that direction a magnitude additionally requires an [[Inner Product]] or norm and a normalization of the generators.

For vector fields $X$ and $Y$,
$$
[X,Y](f)=X(Y(f))-Y(X(f)).
$$
For matrix generators it is the matrix commutator
$$
[X,Y]=XY-YX.
$$

If $\Phi_X^\varepsilon$ and $\Phi_Y^\varepsilon$ are the small flows generated by $X$ and $Y$, the commutator loop
$$
\Phi_X^\varepsilon\Phi_Y^\varepsilon
\Phi_X^{-\varepsilon}\Phi_Y^{-\varepsilon}
$$
produces an $O(\varepsilon^2)$ displacement in the $[X,Y]$ direction. Equivalently,
$$
e^{\varepsilon X}e^{\varepsilon Y}
=\exp\!\left(\varepsilon(X+Y)+\tfrac12\varepsilon^2[X,Y]+O(\varepsilon^3)\right).
$$

This makes brackets useful for:

- predicting operation-order and splitting-method errors
- finding indirectly reachable directions in control systems
- describing how one symmetry transforms another
- testing whether a [[Subalgebra]] or [[Ideal (Lie Algebra)|ideal]] is closed

The bracket is bilinear and antisymmetric and obeys the [[Jacobi Identity]]. Those properties define a [[Lie Algebra]].

In the Gaussian location-scale example, $[D,T]=T$: scaling followed by translation differs from the reverse order by another translation. See [[Affine Group]] and [[Information Geometry]].
