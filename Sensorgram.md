Produced by methods such as [[Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR)]] and [[Biolayer Interferometry (BLI)]]








Ambiguous signal sensor gram

Example 1: Curve with no dissociation![[fig7_pdl1nano_17163_SPR.png]]
In the above sensorgram, the SPR measurement performed showed almost no signal loss during the dissociation phase. Fitting equations returns a strong value, but it does not correspond to the binding affinity.
For instance, this lack of dissociation could arise if too many designs are attached to the plate, allowing the target to release from one design and rebind to a neighboring on


Example 2: Flipped assay disconfirms nanomolar binding![[fig5_LO-CSF1-MP-IF-17_BLI.png]]![[fig6_LO-CSF1-MP-IF-17_BLI-inverted.png]]
Fitting binding affinity equations returns an apparent 20 nM binding strength, which would be a good binder. We then ran a confirmatory assay with the roles reversed, attaching the target to the surface and flowing the binder across it. The new data show an affinity likely no stronger than 5 µM, more than two orders of magnitude weaker




**Example 3: Binder has two dissociation curves instead of one**![[fig8_3ch4nano_15616_SPR.png]]
In some cases, two distinct dissociation curves can be observed in a sensorgram. In this example, the signal first falls quickly before a second phase of slower decrease. It is unclear which phase of the dissociation corresponds to one-to-one binding between the target and the design. Fitting a single exponential across the two events seen in the curve still returns a value, but it does not correspond to any real KD and normally serves to artificially boost the reported affinity.

The sensorgram, therefore, does not yet establish that meaningful binding occurs, and further assays are necessary to confirm. In this instance, we opted for a flipped assay where the roles of target and design are inverted: we attach the target on the surface and flow the binder over it in solution. The resulting sensorgrams paint a clearer picture, revealing that binding is indeed present.
