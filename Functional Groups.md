A specific group of atoms within a molecule responsible for that molecule's characteristic chemical reactions — the same functional group behaves similarly (reacts the same way, has similar properties) no matter what larger molecule it's attached to, which is what makes organic chemistry tractable at all: classify by functional group rather than by the astronomically large number of individual molecules.

**Common examples**
- Hydroxyl (–OH): alcohols
- Carbonyl (C=O): aldehydes/ketones
- Carboxyl (–COOH): carboxylic acids
- Amine (–NH₂): amines — basic, proton-accepting
- Aromatic ring (e.g. [[Benzene]], a 6-carbon ring with delocalized π-electrons): a functional group in its own right, and also a building block other functional groups fuse onto

**Fused-ring functional groups**
Rings can fuse together to form larger, still-classifiable functional groups:
- [[Pyridine]]: a 6-membered aromatic ring with one nitrogen substituted for a carbon (basic, like an amine, because of that nitrogen's lone pair)
- [[Quinoline]] ($C_9H_7N$): pyridine fused to [[Benzene]] — the core [[Alkaloid|alkaloid]] scaffold found in [[Quinine]] and related antimalarial compounds. The fused-ring structure is rigid and aromatic-stabilized, which is part of why alkaloids built on it are chemically stable enough to survive as drugs.

**Why quinine specifically is an alkaloid**
An [[Alkaloid]] is a naturally-occurring compound built around a basic nitrogen (from an amine-like functional group, here the pyridine nitrogen) — that basicity is exactly the chemical handle plant biosynthesis pathways and, later, extraction/purification chemistry both work with.
