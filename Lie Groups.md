A Lie group is a set $G$ with two compatible structures:

1. a [[Groups|group]] structure, with associative multiplication, identity, and inverses
2. a [[Smooth Manifold]] structure for which multiplication and inversion are smooth maps

Examples:

- $(\mathbb R^n,+)$: translations, with dimension $n$
- $S^1=U(1)$: unit complex numbers, with dimension $1$
- $GL_n(\mathbb R)$: invertible matrices, with dimension $n^2$
- $SO(3)$: rotations of $\mathbb R^3$, with dimension $3$
- $SU(2)$: unit quaternions and the double cover of $SO(3)$
- $\mathrm{Aff}^+(1)$: orientation-preserving [[Affine Group|affine transformations]] of the line

**The induced [[Lie Algebra]]**

Start with
$$
\mathfrak g=T_eG,
$$
the [[Tangent Space]] at the identity. A vector $X\in\mathfrak g$ is an infinitesimal group transformation.

Left translation $L_g(h)=gh$ spreads $X$ to a left-invariant vector field
$$
\widetilde X_g=(dL_g)_eX.
$$
The ordinary vector-field bracket then defines
$$
[X,Y]=[\widetilde X,\widetilde Y]_e.
$$
This [[Lie Bracket]] captures the leading-order failure of the flows generated by $X$ and $Y$ to commute. For matrix groups it reduces to
$$
[X,Y]=XY-YX.
$$

The exponential map
$$
\exp:\mathfrak g\to G
$$
integrates an infinitesimal generator into a one-parameter subgroup. For matrix groups it is the matrix exponential. Near the identity, the group and its Lie algebra determine the same local transformation structure, but the algebra is linear and usually easier to compute with.

**Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff: the group ↔ algebra connection**

The exponential map is a bijection near $e$, so the group product pulls back to an operation on the algebra. Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff (BCH) gives that operation as a series built *entirely from iterated [[Lie Bracket|brackets]]*:
$$
\log\!\big(e^{X}e^{Y}\big)
=X+Y+\tfrac12[X,Y]
+\tfrac1{12}\big([X,[X,Y]]+[Y,[Y,X]]\big)
-\tfrac1{24}\,[Y,[X,[X,Y]]]+\cdots
$$
Every term past $X+Y$ is a bracket, so the Lie algebra alone determines group multiplication in a neighborhood of the identity — this is the precise sense in which the linear object reconstructs the curved one. Truncating gives usable approximations for small generators:
$$
e^{X}e^{Y}\approx\exp\!\big(X+Y+\tfrac12[X,Y]\big).
$$
If $[X,Y]=0$ the series collapses to $e^{X}e^{Y}=e^{X+Y}$ (the abelian/commuting case); the first correction $\tfrac12[X,Y]$ is exactly the leading-order failure to commute. See [[Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff]] and the commutator-loop picture in [[Lie Bracket]].

A Lie group can affect another space through a [[Group Actions|group action]]. Differentiating the action turns Lie-algebra elements into vector fields on that space. This is the connection used when the affine group acts on the Gaussian family in [[Information Geometry]].
